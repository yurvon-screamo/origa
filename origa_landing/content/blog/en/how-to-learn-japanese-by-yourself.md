# How to Learn Japanese by Yourself: The First 30 Days and the Loop That Follows

Self-taught Japanese does not fail for lack of talent or apps. It fails because a classroom, whatever its flaws, ships with a structure self-learners have to build themselves: a sequence, deadlines, and feedback. Miss all three and motivation carries you for six weeks, then the anki queue hits 400 cards and the textbook closes.

This page is about building that structure yourself: one study loop that works from month one to fluency, and a concrete plan for the first 30 days. If you have not picked a starting sequence yet, [the realistic zero-to-start plan](/blog/yaponskiy-s-nulya) covers what to learn in what order; this page assumes that question is settled and asks how to keep it running.

## The loop: read, capture, review, use

Everything after the first month is a cycle of four moves:

1. **Read** (or listen) to Japanese slightly above your comfort level. Textbook dialogues at first, then graded readers, manga, subtitles. This is where new material enters; without regular input the rest of the loop starves.
2. **Capture** what you met and want to keep: words, kanji, grammar points, sentences. Friction matters here; a capture step that takes four apps dies within weeks.
3. **Review** the captured items on a schedule. Spaced repetition is the engine of retention, and it only works as a daily habit, not a weekend marathon.
4. **Use** what you reviewed: write a sentence, say it, read something where it appears again. Use is what turns "recognized" into "known."

Classrooms run this loop with a teacher in the middle. Self-learners who succeed run it with tools and habits. Self-learners who fail usually run steps 1 and 3 only, reading passively and grinding flashcards, then wonder why nothing connects.

The loop also answers the motivation question. You do not need willpower for a year; you need the next session to be obvious. Same time, same place, same first action.

## The first 30 days, week by week

**Week 1: kana, both of them.** Hiragana and katakana to recognition level. [The kana guide](/blog/learn-hiragana-katakana) lays out the track. Do not aim for perfection; aim for "reading kana slowly without a chart," and let later weeks polish.

**Week 2: the first 50 words and the sounds.** Basic greetings, numbers, everyday nouns, with audio, so the words land with their sounds and not just their spellings. Start the review habit now: ten minutes, every day, no exceptions. The habit matters more than the volume.

**Week 3: basic grammar through one source.** One textbook, one video series, or one app, chosen and stuck with. です/ます sentences, particles, question forms. The rule is not "the best resource"; it is "one resource, finished."

**Week 4: the first real material.** Something made for natives or learners one step above you: a graded reader passage, a manga page with furigana, a dialogue transcript. Meet the loop in miniature: read it, capture five words from it, review them, read it again two days later and feel the difference.

Thirty days in, you have kana, a core vocabulary seed, sentence skeletons, and, above all, a running loop. From here the inputs scale up; the loop does not change.

## What keeps self-learners going

Three practices separate the ones who continue from the ones who restart every January:

- **A fixed daily floor, not a ceiling.** Twenty minutes every day beats three hours on Sunday. On bad days, do the reviews only and call it done; the streak survives, and the streak is the asset.
- **Material you actually want to read.** Manga, game text, song lyrics, cooking blogs. The "right" resource you avoid is worth less than the "wrong" one you open nightly. [Learning through manga](/blog/learn-japanese-from-manga) and [through anime](/blog/learn-japanese-from-anime) cover the two most common cases.
- **A visible horizon.** A test date works: [JLPT 2026 sessions and registration](/blog/jlpt-2026-dates) give the loop a deadline and a certificate at the end. If exams are not your thing, a concrete project (read volume one unassisted, write a diary entry a day) serves the same role.

## FAQ

### Can you really learn Japanese alone?

To a functional reading and conversational level, yes; thousands do. What self-study lacks, corrective feedback on speaking and writing, is solvable later with language exchange or tutoring sessions layered on top of a self-built base.

### How many hours a day do I need?

A daily floor of 20–30 sustained minutes outperforms rare long sessions. More time accelerates, but the failure mode of self-study is inconsistency, not insufficiency.

### What should I learn first: grammar or vocabulary?

Both, in small interleaved doses, with kana before either. Vocabulary without structure produces phrase-strings; grammar without vocabulary produces empty patterns. Weeks two and three above deliberately overlap them.

### Do I need to take the JLPT?

No, but a level test is a useful horizon: it converts "someday fluent" into a dated, graded target. N5 after a few months of consistent study is realistic, and [the exam dates page](/blog/jlpt-2026-dates) tracks the windows.
